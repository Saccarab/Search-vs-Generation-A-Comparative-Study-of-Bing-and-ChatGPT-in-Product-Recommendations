  LLMs are rapidly becoming the interface through which consumers discover products. By mid-2025, ChatGPT alone had reached approximately 10\% of the world's adult population, with information-seeking and practical guidance accounting for nearly 80\% of all conversations \parencite{chatterji2025chatgpt}. Yet especially for commercial intent prompts these models do not generate recommendations from memory alone anymore. When a user asks for a product recommendation, the system can search the web in real time, retrieve relevant sources, and ground its response in what
  it finds. This retrieval-and-grounding workflow is commonly referred to as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and it is
  increasingly standard in LLM conversations when up-to-date information is required. The model supplies the reasoning; the web supplies the knowledge. This architecture is
  not a transitional compromise but the economically and technically inevitable design: inference is expensive, retrieval is
  cheap, and no model can be retrained hourly to keep pace with prices, reviews, and product launches.

  If retrieval is the foundation, then what gets retrieved determines what gets recommended. And what gets retrieved is
  governed, overwhelmingly, by search engine indices. This thesis provides the empirical evidence for that claim. We trace the
  full retrieval-to-citation pipeline, from fan-out query generation through candidate retrieval, source selection, and
  claim-level attribution, and show that the vast majority of LLM citations originate from conventional search results.
  Despite growing industry speculation that generative AI will render traditional search optimization obsolete, we will argue in this thesis that Search Engine
  Optimization (SEO) is not dead; it is the prerequisite.

  But even if SEO is the prerequisite, is it sufficient? If it is not, content creators would need to optimize for both search engines and LLM-specific preferences. We investigate this question by examining grounding behavior as the measurable pipeline from retrieval through selection, citation, and final text generation: which sources the system retrieves, which survive into the response, how claims map to sources, and whether the cited content is faithfully reproduced.

Grounding is more likely to happen when the user's intent demands real-time, verifiable information (prices, availability, feature comparisons, reviews, local information) that parametric knowledge cannot reliably provide. \subsection{The Emerging GEO Industry}
\label{sec:geo-landscape}

The commercial significance of LLM citation behavior has already attracted substantial venture capital. In August 2025, Profound raised \$35~million in a Series~B round led by Sequoia Capital, bringing its total funding to \$58.5~million \parencite{fortune2025profound}. Three months later, Berlin-based Peec~AI raised \$21~million in a Series~A led by Singular \parencite{techcrunch2025peec}, growing to \$4M~ARR in 10~months with 1,300 companies on its platform. Both companies are building brand-visibility dashboards that track how often a brand appears in LLM-generated responses aand track the sentiment AI agents use when mentioning brands.

Ahrefs, one of the largest SEO analytics platforms, has also added AI search monitoring features that track brand visibility across LLM-generated responses alongside its traditional SERP tracking. Adobe announced the acquisition of SEMrush in November 2025 \parencite{adobe2025semrush}, a deal that signals how seriously the marketing technology industry takes the convergence of traditional keyword intelligence and AI search analytics. At their core, these platforms are trying to answer a simple question: \emph{is the AI recommending your brand, and if not, why?} Our findings speak directly to what drives these outcomes. 


The dominant industry narrative frames GEO as a replacement for SEO, a new discipline for a new era. Some industry practitioners have suggested that LLM-referred users may exhibit higher conversion rates and purchase intent than traditional search traffic, lending urgency to the claim. However, the empirical question this thesis investigates is whether GEO is genuinely distinct from SEO or whether it is, in practice, a thin optimization layer on top of the same ranking foundation. The answer, as the following chapters demonstrate, is substantially the latter, but with empirically real exceptions that the concluding chapter delineates (Section~\ref{sec:geo-not-seo}).

\section{Background}
\label{sec:theoretical-framework}

\subsection{From SEO to GEO}
\label{sec:evolution-search}
Search Engine Optimization (SEO) is the established practice of optimizing content for visibility in organic search results through content, external website link-based authority signals, and technical performance. As LLMs increasingly mediate search interactions, a similar optimization practice is emerging. The industry has not converged on a single term for this; common labels include GEO (Generative Engine Optimization), AEO (Answer Engine Optimization), AI~SEO and AIO (AI Optimization). Throughout this thesis we adopt \textbf{GEO} following \textcite{aggarwal2024geo}, as it is the most established term in academic literature, but the underlying concept is the same across labels: optimizing content so that it survives the retrieval-to-citation pipeline. Understanding why GEO exists, and why it does not replace SEO, requires tracing the architectural evolution that made retrieval central to how LLMs generate answers.

\subsection{The Rise of Retrieval-Augmented Generation}
\label{sec:rag-evolution}

Early LLMs relied purely on parametric knowledge: static information frozen at the time of training. This produced two well-documented failure modes: hallucination (confident generation of false claims) and data staleness (inability to reflect prices, reviews, or product launches that post-date the training cutoff). The fundamental insight that led to Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) was that LLMs are better at \emph{reasoning} (logic, synthesis, formatting) than at being a database \parencite{lewis2020rag}. Rather than encoding all world knowledge in model weights, RAG decouples reasoning from knowledge by introducing a three-stage pipeline: the model first \emph{retrieves} relevant documents from an external source (typically a search engine), the retrieved text is \emph{augmented} into the model's context window as grounding chunks, and the model then \emph{generates} a response by reasoning over the provided evidence.

RAG became the industry standard for three reinforcing reasons. First, it provides a citation trail: every claim can be traced to a retrievable source, enabling at least partial verification. Second, it is efficient: smaller models augmented with retrieval routinely outperform larger models operating from parametric knowledge alone, because the retrieval step offloads the burden of memorization. Third, it is the only scalable mechanism for freshness: no model can be retrained hourly to keep pace with the web, but a search index can be queried in real time.

This vision is not aspirational; it is economically inevitable. Inference (the computational cost of LLM ``thinking'') is orders of magnitude more expensive than retrieval via traditional search indexing. It also cannot produce as fresh results as the web that changes by the minute. Retrieval, by contrast, is cheap, real-time. The economic logic is clear: let the model reason, and let the search index remember. The practical consequence for this thesis is equally clear: if retrieval is the foundation of AI-generated answers, then what gets retrieved determines what gets recommended.

\subsection{From Search to Grounding}
\label{sec:structural-pivot}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

Research on how LLMs select and cite web sources is young (most production RAG systems with grounding launched in 2024--2025), and the empirical literature remains thin. The work closest to ours falls into three categories: citation fidelity audits, large-scale observational studies of citation patterns, and investigations of the grounding mechanics that sit between retrieval and generation. We review each in turn, highlighting the specific gaps this thesis addresses.

\subsection{Citation Fidelity in RAG Systems}
\label{sec:rw-fidelity}

The question of whether RAG-grounded LLMs actually cite what they claim to cite has received limited but rigorous attention. \textcite{wu2025sourcecheckup} developed SourceCheckup, an automated verification framework that evaluated 7~LLMs across 800~medical questions, generating 58,000 statement--source pairs validated by physicians. Their central finding is that retrieval substantially reduces citation errors: GPT-4o with web search achieved $\sim$70\% statement-level support versus substantially worse rates for parametric-only models. Our study extends this line of inquiry into a different domain (commercial product recommendations) on newer model generations (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5), with a narrower but deeper focus: rather than scoring all statements in a response, we audit individual product claims against their solo-cited listicle sources and manually verify every flagged case (Section~\ref{sec:listicle-bias}).

\subsection{Large-Scale Observational Studies}
\label{sec:rw-observational}

Two practitioner studies provide the largest-scale empirical data on ChatGPT citation behavior. \textcite{punturo2026citationsources} analyzed 730,000 U.S.\ English-language ChatGPT conversations (October--December 2025), reporting that only 18\% of conversations trigger web search overall. However, the trigger rate varies sharply by intent: commercial queries (product comparisons, purchase advice, ``best X for Y'' questions) trigger web search at 53.5\%, roughly 3$\times$ the all-intent baseline (see Section~\ref{sec:commercial-catalyst} and Figure~\ref{fig:trigger-rate} for a detailed breakdown). The same study found approximately 4 unique citations per search-triggered turn and a Gini coefficient of 0.8 for domain concentration, indicating that a small number of domains capture the bulk of citations. \textcite{rudzki2026english} analyzed 10M+ ChatGPT prompts and 20M+ fan-out queries via Peec~AI's production monitoring, finding that 78\% of non-English sessions include at least one English sub-search and 43\% of all fan-out queries are issued in English regardless of the user's language.

We use controlled, repeated prompts across ChatGPT Business, ChatGPT Personal, and Gemini with full network instrumentation, enabling us to trace each step from fan-out query through retrieved candidates to cited set and claim-level attribution (see Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} for full design). Our higher search trigger rate (89\% vs.\ their 18\% baseline) and citation density (9.94 vs.\ $\sim$4 per turn) reflect deliberate prompt design for high commercial intent rather than a sampling difference; Profound's own intent-stratified data confirms that commercial queries trigger search at 53.5\%, already 3$\times$ the all-intent baseline. The cross-language fan-out patterns documented by \textcite{rudzki2026english} (Case~1: foreign prompt $\rightarrow$ English retrieval) are directly corroborated in our data; we additionally identify the reverse direction (Cases~2--3: English prompt $\rightarrow$ foreign-language retrieval), which has not been previously documented (Section~\ref{sec:localization-bias}).

\subsection{Grounding Mechanics and the Content Budget}
\label{sec:rw-grounding-mechanics}

A growing body of practitioner research examines \emph{how much} of a retrieved page actually enters the model's context. \textcite{petrovic2025grounding} measured Gemini's grounding chunks and found that the system operates with a fixed content budget of approximately 2,000 words per query, with the top-ranked source receiving 28\% of the budget and pages over 3,000 words achieving only 13\% coverage. \textcite{petrovic2025filter} further quantified the compression ratio between retrieved content and generated output: only 15--25\% of retrieved text survives into the final response. \textcite{indig2026attention} analyzed 1.2~million ChatGPT responses and found a ``ski ramp'' distribution where 44.2\% of citations originate from the first 30\% of a source page ($p < 0.0001$ over 18,012 verified citations), with citation likelihood correlating with definitive language and entity density.

These findings are directly relevant to our within-listicle position bias results (Section~\ref{sec:reranking}): if the model's grounding window is finite and front-loaded, items listed later in a long listicle may never enter the content the model processes, which may help explain why the \#1 listicle item is selected at 1.7--2.5$\times$ the uniform baseline. While we do not measure grounding budgets directly, our study provides complementary evidence from the selection side: we observe the downstream effect (position-dependent uptake rates) that these budget constraints appear to produce.

\subsection{The ``Agent Web'' and Format Optimization}
\label{sec:rw-agent-web}

A parallel line of work asks whether serving content in machine-optimized formats improves AI visibility. \textcite{martinho2026markdown} describe Cloudflare's ``Markdown for Agents'' feature, which uses HTTP content negotiation to serve markdown instead of HTML when AI crawlers request a page, reducing token consumption by up to 80\%. The \texttt{llms.txt} proposal \parencite{howard2024llmstxt}, a markdown file providing LLMs with a curated site map, has been adopted by approximately 800,000 websites, though an empirical study of 300,000 domains by SE~Ranking found no correlation between \texttt{llms.txt} presence and AI citation frequency \parencite{seranking2025llmstxt}. \textcite{punturo2026markdown} tested the markdown hypothesis directly in a controlled A/B experiment across 381~pages and found no statistically significant advantage for markdown over standard HTML in AI bot traffic.

These null results are informative: they suggest that current LLMs are already effective at extracting structure from HTML, and that the ``Agent Web'' may be more about infrastructure efficiency than content visibility. Our Content DNA analysis (Section~\ref{sec:selection-drift}) approaches the question from the opposite direction: rather than asking whether \emph{format} matters, we ask whether \emph{structural content features} (tables, numbered lists, pros/cons sections) predict citation. The answer is yes, with measurable drift signals, suggesting that what matters is the \emph{information architecture} of a page rather than its serialization format.

\textbf{Summary of gaps this thesis addresses}: Prior work has audited citation fidelity (but not selection mechanics), measured citation patterns at scale (but without access to retrieval pipelines), quantified grounding budgets (but not the downstream selection effects), and tested format optimization (but not structural content features). This thesis attempts to connect these threads by tracing the retrieval-to-citation pipeline across multiple LLMs and tiers (ChatGPT Business vs.\ Personal, and Gemini), with particular attention to within-listicle position bias, citations that fall outside search indices entirely, and claim-level attribution.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

\textit{RQ1: How does grounding behavior manifest in LLM-generated product recommendations, and how does it vary across deployment tiers and providers?}

This core question decomposes into four sub-questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item RQ1a (Selection and visibility): How do cited, additional, rejected, and invisible sources differ in domain and type, and how does this differ between Business and Personal tiers?
    \item RQ1b (External support): How often do LLM citations appear in conventional search engine results (Bing and Google)?
    \item RQ1c (Selection bias): Do models exhibit preferences for specific structural content features (e.g., tables, numbered lists, freshness cues) when selecting from the retrieved pool, and how do these preferences vary across systems?
    \item RQ1d (Listicle fidelity): When listicles (list-format articles or blog posts such as "Top 10 Best...") are cited, which products within them are selected or ignored, and how faithfully does the model reproduce their details?
\end{enumerate}

To answer these questions, we compare LLM citations against two external search indices. Bing serves as the primary overlap baseline for ChatGPT, while Google provides a second overlap index and the retrieval baseline for Gemini. Then we analyze the characteristics of the pages that made it to the citations sections of these LLM responses and compare them against the ones that did not.
