\subsection{Selection of the Research Query Set}
\label{sec:query-set-selection}

Our dataset consists of 79 unique product recommendation prompts, sourced from two platforms. The majority---65 prompts---were drawn from Profound's Prompt Volumes feature, which provides prompt-level data derived from licensed, double-opt-in consumer panels of real ChatGPT users. Data is anonymized, aggregated, scrubbed of PII, and GDPR/CCPA compliant; Profound states that surfaced prompts may be rewritten for anonymity or clarity, so these are representative of real user intent rather than verbatim transcripts \parencite{profound2026promptvolumes}. The remaining 14 prompts were identified through Ahrefs' AI Citations feature, which tracks URLs and domains appearing in ChatGPT responses; we used it to surface additional high-intent prompts within our target verticals. Prompts span collection dates from January 2025 to January 2026, with the majority concentrated between July and December 2025.

From the broad set of available prompts, we deliberately filtered for high commercial intent---product recommendations, comparisons, and ``best of'' queries---to ensure the study reflects the segment of search where RAG is most active and where the retrieval-to-citation pipeline is most empirically observable (see Section~\ref{sec:commercial-catalyst}). All queries were concentrated in the AI and Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) sectors, particularly speech and language technology (speech-to-text, live transcription, voice translation, text-to-speech). This domain focus was a deliberate methodological choice: the author's professional expertise in this vertical enabled more nuanced qualitative analysis of retrieval relevance and citation quality. Representative examples include: \emph{``Which free AI would you recommend for translating my video?''} (P001), \emph{``Is there a transcription software available for a one-time payment instead of a subscription?''} (P003), \emph{``Are there live translation software options for video chats like Zoom or Teams?''} (P044), and \emph{``Can you recommend free text-to-speech extensions that don't sound robotic?''} (P069).

Each of the 79 prompts was executed in 3 independent runs per deployment tier (with a 4th run added only in cases of technical failure or RAG non-triggering), yielding 474 ChatGPT runs (237 Business + 237 Personal) and 237 Gemini runs---711 total runs across all conditions.

\paragraph{Intent-based prompt clusters.}
Although the 79 prompts span 9 fine-grained \texttt{parent\_keyword} categories, they reduce to 4 functional clusters when grouped by user intent: \textbf{live/real-time} (25 prompts, 31.6\%), \textbf{post-production translation} (24, 30.4\%), \textbf{post-production transcription/STT} (21, 26.6\%), and \textbf{text-to-speech} (9, 11.4\%). The first three clusters are roughly balanced; TTS forms a smaller tail. Representative examples include: \emph{``Is there a Windows application for live translation during calls in Google Meet?''} (live), \emph{``Which free AI would you recommend for translating my video?''} (post-prod translation), \emph{``What are the best free audio to text transcription tools?''} (transcription/STT), and \emph{``Can you recommend free text-to-speech extensions that don't sound robotic?''} (TTS).

\paragraph{Search trigger coverage.}
Five prompts never triggered RAG in any of their 6 runs (P013, P036, P056, P059, P062); a sixth (P067) also never triggered. All 6 have \texttt{NULL} sonic classifier data across every run --- the classifier was never invoked, indicating suppression at a layer upstream of the classifier itself. Two are non-English (P056~Italian, P062~Spanish). Among runs with valid sonic data, the classifier is \textbf{deterministic}: all 3 runs of the same prompt on the same account produce byte-identical probability values, confirming that run-to-run stochasticity originates in post-classifier layers. Thirteen runs exhibited post-classifier overrides (high \texttt{simple\_search\_prob} but search suppressed); 11 of 13 occurred on Personal accounts. All downstream citation analyses exclude non-triggering runs.

\section{Anatomy of a ChatGPT Response}
\label{sec:chatgpt-anatomy}

This section describes what we can observe about ChatGPT's retrieval and citation pipeline from captured network payloads, and the naming conventions and field definitions that structure our analysis.

\subsection{Pipeline Overview}
\label{sec:pipeline-overview}

Our network instrumentation captured raw event-stream payloads from the ChatGPT production UI (\texttt{chatgpt.com}) using the Chrome DevTools protocol. The payloads reveal a three-layer pipeline---Sonic Classifier, Sonicberry Orchestrator, and Generation Model---rather than a monolithic model. The detailed architecture, layer descriptions, and observed flow are reported as empirical findings in Section~\ref{sec:three-layer}. The methodological implication is that because each layer emits distinct payload fields, we can separately instrument the search-trigger decision, the fan-out query dispatch, and the citation-generation phase.

\subsection{Source Categories (Naming Conventions)}
\label{sec:source-categories}

We classify every URL that appears in ChatGPT's network response into one of three categories, derived from distinct locations in the payload. \textbf{Cited} URLs appear as inline footnote references in the generated answer; mechanically, these are URLs linked via \texttt{content\_references} token spans, rendering as numbered superscript links in the ChatGPT UI. \textbf{Additional} URLs are attached to the response but not referenced inline; they appear in the \texttt{sources\_additional} array and render as a collapsible ``Sources'' section below the main response. \textbf{Retrieved-only} URLs are present in the \texttt{search\_result\_groups} payload (the raw search results delivered to the model during generation) but were not promoted to either cited or additional status---they never appear in the user-facing response. We compute them as: all URLs in \texttt{search\_result\_groups} minus those already classified as cited or additional (see Section~\ref{sec:retrieved-only} for detailed analysis).

The \texttt{search\_result\_groups} field represents the retrieval pool as it appears in ChatGPT's network stream. We do not know the exact upstream source of these results; while ChatGPT is documented to use Bing, the field itself does not identify the backing search provider, and we cannot rule out internal indices or other retrieval paths.

\subsection{Search Trigger Instrumentation}
\label{sec:search-trigger-method}

We log the system's search-decision artifacts where present in the event stream: the \texttt{sonic\_classification\_result} (classifier output with \texttt{simple\_search\_prob}, \texttt{complex\_search\_prob}, and thresholds), \texttt{web\_search\_triggered} (whether search actually ran), and \texttt{web\_search\_forced} (whether search was forced). Business streams are the richest source of these fields. The full Sonic Classifier configuration and its empirical behavior are reported in Section~\ref{sec:search-trigger}.

\subsection{Fan-Out Queries and the \texttt{web.run} Agentic Loop}
\label{sec:fan-out-queries}

Fan-out queries are the multiple search queries ChatGPT issues for a single user prompt, reshaping and expanding the retrieval scope. They are the gate that determines which sources are even eligible to be cited. We observe them via the \texttt{search\_model\_queries.queries} field in the network stream. [maybe give an example based on our urns?]

Our instrumentation revealed that the generation model (gpt-5-2) calls a tool named \texttt{web.run} via the Sonicberry orchestrator. Each \texttt{web.run} call that dispatches exactly 2 queries in parallel. In 94\% of runs, a single \texttt{web.run} call suffices. In 2.7\% of runs, the generation model evaluates the returned results and calls \texttt{web.run} again, creating an iterative re-search loop tracked by the \texttt{search\_turns\_count} field. In the raw network stream, each \texttt{web.run} message carries the query array (always length~2) and an incrementing search turn count; multi-turn calls may be chained (\texttt{parent\_id} linking to the previous call) or independent. The empirical distribution of search turns, re-search strategies, and query drift are reported in Section~\ref{sec:multi-turn}.

\subsection{Retrieved Candidate Pool}
\label{sec:retrieved-candidates}

The \texttt{search\_result\_groups} field contains the full retrieval pool delivered to the model: grouped search entries with URL, title, snippet, and \texttt{ref\_id} (turn/reference index keys). This is the model's ``shortlist menu'' prior to selection---every URL that the model could have cited or attached.

\subsection{Citation Tokens and Claim Mapping}
\label{sec:citation-tokens}

Two payload elements enable claim-to-source reconstruction: \texttt{content\_references} (citation token spans with \texttt{start\_idx}/\texttt{end\_idx} marking where citations appear in the output stream) and the streamed \texttt{response\_text}. From these, we extract claim blocks (the text segments preceding each citation token) and map them to their backing sources by resolving \texttt{ref\_id} keys against \texttt{search\_result\_groups}. This reconstruction pipeline is described in detail in Section~\ref{sec:claim-to-link}.

\subsection{Limitations of Network Instrumentation}
\label{sec:network-limitations}

The network stream does not include the exact on-page snippets or chunks that were fetched and inserted into the model's context window. Our ChatGPT-side grounding analyses are therefore output-side proxies (claim-attributed text), not true input-side snippet budgets. \subsection{Network Parameter Glossary (ChatGPT)}
\label{sec:network-glossary}

\textit{A practical glossary of the recurring fields we use when decoding the raw event stream. This is intentionally limited to parameters we directly observed in captured payloads.}

\subsubsection{Identifiers / linkage}
\label{sec:glossary-identifiers}

\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{conversation\_id}] Conversation session identifier (useful for grouping, not analysis).
    \item[\texttt{message.id}] Unique message identifier in the stream.
    \item[\texttt{parent\_id}] Links a message to its parent; helps follow the chain of events.
    \item[\texttt{request\_id}] Correlates events belonging to the same request; often shared across tool calls and patches for that run.
    \item[\texttt{turn\_exchange\_id} / \texttt{turn\_trace\_id}] Internal tracing IDs for a single turn; useful for debugging continuity across events.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Search trigger \& decision}
\label{sec:glossary-search-trigger}

\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{sonic\_classification\_result}] The search trigger classifier output (probabilities + thresholds).
    \item[\texttt{web\_search\_triggered} / \texttt{web\_search\_forced}] Whether search ran / was forced (when present).
    \item[\texttt{message\_marker}] Markers like \texttt{search\_start} that indicate when retrieval begins.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Fan-out queries \& search turns}
\label{sec:glossary-fan-out}

\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{metadata.search\_model\_queries.queries[]}] The model-generated fan-out query list for a search turn (our ``hidden queries'').
    \item[\texttt{search\_turns\_count}] Which search turn we are in (1, 2, 3\ldots); multi-turn fan-out appears as repeated query batches with increasing counts.
    \item[\texttt{search\_tool\_call\_count}] Run-level count of search tool invocations; typically correlates with \texttt{max(search\_turns\_count)} (single-turn: 1; multi-turn: 2+).
    \item[\texttt{search\_source} / \texttt{client\_reported\_search\_source}] Indicates the origin mode (often \texttt{composer\_auto}); useful for auditing when behavior changes.

\end{description}

\subsubsection{Retrieved candidates (what came back from search)}
\label{sec:glossary-retrieved}

\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{metadata.search\_result\_groups}] The grouped retrieval pool (URLs, titles, snippets).
    \item[\texttt{ref\_id}] The key (turn/ref indices) we use to map candidates to citations (\texttt{turn\_index}, \texttt{ref\_type}, \texttt{ref\_index}).
    \item[\texttt{debug\_sonic\_thread\_id}] Internal identifier for the search thread (debugging/trace only).
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Citation / attribution artifacts}
\label{sec:glossary-citation}

\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{content\_references}] Citation token spans inserted into the streamed response; indices point to the citation token location, not the claim boundary.
    \item[\texttt{safe\_urls} and \texttt{url\_moderation} events] Safety/allow-list decisions for URLs that show up in citations and link cards.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Completion / response framing}
\label{sec:glossary-completion}

\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{finish\_details}] How generation ended (stop reason / tokens).
    \item[\texttt{model\_slug} / \texttt{default\_model\_slug}] The model variant used for the run.
\end{description}

\section{Anatomy of a Gemini Response}
\label{sec:gemini-anatomy}

\textit{How the Gemini Vertex AI API exposes grounding metadata compared to ChatGPT's hidden network stream.}

\subsection{The \texttt{groundingMetadata} Schema}
\label{sec:grounding-metadata}

Unlike ChatGPT, where we must ``scrape'' the network stream, Gemini provides structured grounding data in the API response:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{webSearchQueries}}: The explicit fan-out query set generated by the model.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{groundingChunks}}: The list of web sources used for grounding, each containing a redirect URL and a domain-level title (e.g., \texttt{heygen.com})---but \emph{not} the raw page text itself.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{groundingSupports}}: The precise mapping between specific segments of the response and the \texttt{groundingChunks} (the ``paper trail'').
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Key Differences in Instrumentation}
\label{sec:gemini-instrumentation-differences}

The most significant difference is transparency: Gemini is ``grounding-first''---it exposes the source URLs it retrieved and maps specific response segments back to those sources via \texttt{groundingSupports}, enabling higher-resolution fidelity analysis than ChatGPT's output-side-only instrumentation allows. However, Gemini does not expose the raw page text it ingested; we see which URLs were used and which response segments they support, but not the actual snippets the model read from each page.

A practical challenge is that Gemini's \texttt{groundingChunks} do not expose raw URLs; instead, they contain internal redirect URLs (e.g., \texttt{vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com/\ldots}) that must be followed to discover the actual destination domain. We built a dedicated resolution script (\texttt{scripts/resolve\_grounding\_urls.mjs}) that collects all unique Vertex redirect URLs, follows each via HTTP, and produces a Vertex URL $\rightarrow$ resolved destination mapping. Redirects that failed programmatically (due to timeouts or anti-bot blocks) were resolved via a custom Chrome extension (\texttt{VertexResolverExtension}) that opens each URL in a background tab and captures the final destination. Without this resolution step, no domain-level or overlap analysis would be possible on the Gemini data.

\section{Search Engine Baselines}
\label{sec:serp-baselines}

With the ChatGPT and Gemini instrumentation established, we now describe the search engine baselines against which we measure citation overlap. These baselines answer the question: are the URLs these models cite also present in conventional search results for the same queries?

\subsection{Google SERP Baseline (SerpApi)}
\label{sec:google-serp-types}

Our Google SERP baseline was collected via SerpApi, which returns results in multiple \texttt{result\_type} buckets: organic results (standard web listings), video results (often YouTube-heavy), and discussion/forum blocks. For each query, we paginated SerpApi until at least 25 organic results were collected---in practice, this typically required approximately 3 pages of results. This ``Top-25 Organic'' baseline serves as the Google control surface against which we measure citation overlap, complementing the deeper Bing Top-200 scrape described below.

\subsection{Bing SERP Scraping (Browser Extension)}
\label{sec:bing-scraping}

Because Microsoft retired public Bing Search API access on August~11, 2025 (see Section~\ref{sec:structural-pivot}), no stable programmatic API was available for academic SERP collection. We therefore used a Chrome browser extension (\texttt{tools/Bing Results Scraper}) that scrapes Bing's live consumer UI via DOM interaction. The extension runs as a Manifest~V3 content script that simulates human search interaction---typing queries character-by-character with randomized inter-key delays (10--50\,ms), clicking the search button, and waiting for DOM stabilization (result count checked across 3 consecutive iterations before proceeding).

Organic results are isolated from ads using a multi-layer filtering approach. The primary selector (\texttt{.b\_algo:not(.b\_adTop):not(.b\_adBottom):not([data-apurl])}) is supplemented by a secondary function that checks DOM class markers, text-prefix matching (\texttt{/\^{}Sponsored\textbackslash b/i}), CSS \texttt{::before} pseudo-element inspection, and URL-based filtering of known ad-click endpoints. Unresolved Bing redirect URLs (\texttt{bing.com/ck/}) are discarded as probable ads. For each result, the extension extracts position, title, URL, domain, display URL, snippet, and page number; Bing's redirect-wrapped URLs are decoded via URL-parameter extraction with Base64 and hex-byte fallback, and tracking parameters (\texttt{utm\_*}, \texttt{fbclid}, \texttt{gclid}) are stripped to normalize URLs for overlap matching.

The extension paginates through 20 pages of results ($\sim$10 organic results per page = Rank~200 per query), with 3-second waits between pages and positions numbered strictly sequentially across pages. All Bing scrapes were routed through a US-based proxy to match the retrieval environment used for ChatGPT runs (see Section~\ref{sec:proxy-requirement}), ensuring that localized SERP variation does not inflate or deflate overlap measurements.

\section{Methodological Evolution: From Top~30 to ``Deep Hunt'' (Rank~200)}
\label{sec:methodological-evolution}

Our scrape depth evolved through three stages as the study uncovered progressively more of ChatGPT's retrieval architecture. The initial design used only the single fan-out query visible in ChatGPT's UI (the ``Searching for\ldots'' text) and scraped the Top~30 Bing results for each, yielding a 40.3\% citation overlap. At this stage we had no knowledge of the hidden double fan-out mechanism---we assumed the UI-displayed query was the only query issued.

The first turning point came with network instrumentation (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline-overview}). Capturing raw event-stream payloads revealed that ChatGPT always dispatches \emph{two} fan-out queries per search turn, not one; the ``Searching for\ldots'' text surfaces only one of them. This meant our initial scrapes were matching against an incomplete query set, systematically understating true overlap. Even after correcting for the double fan-out, qualitative review using our custom Data Viewer revealed a persistent visibility gap: ChatGPT was citing high-quality, relevant pages that were completely absent from the Top~30 human-facing results.

To test whether these citations were truly ``invisible'' or merely ``buried,'' we expanded to a Deep Hunt at Rank~200. Many citations did appear at depth---Bing surfaces the exact pages ChatGPT cites, but at ranks well beyond typical human scrolling. However, the poor relevance and instability of results beyond Page~1 (see Section~\ref{sec:bing-pagination-instability}) suggest that Bing's consumer UI may not maintain a tightly curated ranked list beyond the first page.

Even Rank~200 may not be sufficient. Our page-level distribution data (Section~\ref{sec:page-dist}) shows that citation matches do not reach zero at Pages~15--16; the long tail continues with a smooth decay curve. Extending to Rank~300 or beyond would likely recover additional matches and further narrow the ``truly invisible'' set (see Section~\ref{sec:fw-deeper-crawling}).

In the full scrape (Table~\ref{tab:bing-full}), Page~1 returned 7 tightly relevant video translation tools. In the truncated scrape (Table~\ref{tab:bing-truncated}), the same query returned only \texttt{zeemo.ai} and \texttt{wayin.ai} on Page~1; the remaining 5 results (ranks 3--7, bolded in Table~\ref{tab:bing-full}) were lost entirely---they did not resurface on Page~2 or at any subsequent depth, even when scraping to Rank~200.

Page~2 exhibited two notable patterns. First, a sharp relevance drop: the truncated run's Page~2 included \texttt{a2e.ai} (uncensored AI videos) and \texttt{dreamfaceapp.com} (image generation)---off-topic results that would never appear on a well-curated Page~1. The full run's Page~2 similarly included \texttt{pdftranslate.ai} (PDF translation) and \texttt{lipsync.video} (lip-sync tool)---tangentially related at best. Second, both Page~2 results shared a similar character despite their Page~1 results differing substantially: \texttt{notegpt.io} and \texttt{maestra.ai} appeared on Page~2 in both runs. This suggests that Bing's Page~2 starts from a fixed offset regardless of how many organic results Page~1 actually displayed. From Page~3 onward, continuity resumed with a gradual decay. Our citation-overlap data corroborates this pattern: Page~1 concentrates the bulk of matches, Page~2 shows a conspicuous dip, and Pages~3+ settle into a gradual decay (Section~\ref{sec:page-dist}).

This instability has two consequences for our overlap measurements. First, it introduces a conservative bias: if our scrape captured a truncated Page~1, we may have missed high-ranking results that ChatGPT's backend integration almost certainly had access to, causing us to classify some citations as ``invisible'' when they were in fact present in the index. Second, it is why we needed to scrape to Rank~200---not because ChatGPT is truly ``deep hunting'' at those ranks, but to compensate for UI-layer instability and recover matches that a stable API would have returned in the Top~10--30.

We strongly suspect that ChatGPT does not receive the same noisy, truncated pagination that Bing's web UI serves to human users. Bing likely provides its index to ChatGPT via a backend integration that returns a clean, stable ranked list without ads, carousels, or pagination artifacts. Our Bing-UI-scraped baseline is therefore a degraded proxy for what ChatGPT actually sees, further supporting the interpretation that our overlap figures are conservative lower bounds. This instability was not observed in our Google SERP data collected via SerpApi, which returned consistent, stable organic rankings across paginated requests---an asymmetry worth noting for researchers attempting to replicate grounding studies.

\section{Content DNA Enrichment (LLM-as-a-Labeler)}
\label{sec:content-dna}

To quantify selection effects---what gets cited versus what was available---we needed to move beyond URLs and domains to a structured profile of each page's content characteristics.

\subsection{The Labeling Pipeline}
\label{sec:labeling-pipeline}

We developed an automated enrichment pipeline using GPT-5-mini and Gemini~2.5~Flash as structured labelers. The pipeline operates in three stages. First, raw HTML was fetched for each URL and converted to clean markdown/text. Second, the LLM was prompted to evaluate each page against a structured schema covering page type (\texttt{listicle}, \texttt{product\_page}, \texttt{documentation}, \texttt{forum\_ugc}), content format (\texttt{best\_of\_list}, \texttt{landing\_page}, \texttt{comparison\_matrix}), and structural features (\texttt{has\_tables}, \texttt{has\_numbered\_lists}, \texttt{has\_pros\_cons}). The schema also captured tone and promotional intensity scores, but these showed no discriminative power in downstream analysis---tone distribution was uniformly $\sim$70\% promotional across all systems, a natural consequence of the product-recommendation prompt set---and are omitted from the findings. Third, a subset of labels was manually audited to ensure the labeler correctly distinguished between vendor-owned landing pages and independent editorial listicles.

\subsection{Enrichment Logic and Static Overrides}
\label{sec:enrichment-logic}

The labeling pipeline uses a hybrid approach. General web pages, blogs, and niche product sites were labeled by GPT-5-mini. However, high-volume domains with fixed, well-understood structures were assigned static ``pre-made'' labels to save API quota and ensure consistency: \texttt{reddit.com} was labeled as \texttt{forum\_ugc}, \texttt{en.wikipedia.org} as \texttt{reference}, \texttt{arxiv.org} as \texttt{academic\_paper}, and app stores (\texttt{apps.apple.com}, \texttt{play.google.com}) as \texttt{app\_store\_listing}. Many of the top ``invisible'' domains (Section~\ref{sec:top-invisible-domains}) were handled via these static overrides because their structure does not require per-page LLM analysis.

A consistent skip list (\texttt{wikipedia.org}, \texttt{reddit.com}, \texttt{arxiv.org}, \texttt{github.com}, \texttt{youtube.com}, app stores, social media platforms, and first-party support/docs sites) was applied across all enrichment runs. These domains have fixed, well-understood structures---a Wikipedia article is always \texttt{type=reference}, a Reddit thread is always \texttt{type=forum\_ugc}---and their content is unlikely to contain the structural features (comparison tables, numbered lists, pros/cons blocks) that drive our drift analysis. They were therefore assigned heuristic labels with conservative defaults rather than full LLM enrichment.

\textbf{Consequence for drift analysis:} Because these skipped domains receive zeroed-out structural scores (no tables, no numbered lists, no pros/cons), including them in feature-level drift comparisons would create artificial signal. The selection drift analysis in Section~3.5 therefore focuses on \textbf{product pages and listicles} --- the two dominant types where full LLM enrichment was performed and where structural features are meaningfully variable. This scoping avoids comparing LLM-enriched pages against heuristically-labeled ones, which would conflate labeling method differences with actual content differences.

\subsection{Labeler Fidelity (cross-model agreement)}
\label{sec:labeler-fidelity}

To test whether Content DNA is model-dependent, we ran a direct agreement audit between \textbf{Gemini 2.5 Flash} and \textbf{GPT-5-mini} on \textbf{2,660 overlapping URLs} (\texttt{docs/inter\_model\_fidelity\_report.md}).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structural fields (agreement)}: \texttt{has\_pros\_cons} \textbf{94.6\%}, \texttt{has\_sources\_or\_citations} \textbf{93.8\%}, \texttt{has\_clear\_authorship} \textbf{93.3\%}, \texttt{has\_tables} \textbf{92.9\%}, \texttt{has\_numbered\_lists} \textbf{91.1\%}
    \item \textbf{Categorical fields (agreement)}: \texttt{content\_format} \textbf{92.1\%}, \texttt{type} \textbf{87.7\%}, \texttt{tone} \textbf{77.7\%}
    \item \textbf{Score consistency (correlation)}: \texttt{freshness\_cue\_strength} \textbf{$r = 0.841$} (strong trend agreement, different absolute thresholds)
\end{itemize}

A parallel cross-model validation was performed on the listicle semantic fidelity judge (Section~\ref{sec:semantic-fidelity}), where all 1{,}116 claims were judged by both models. Agreement rates (93--100\% on structural fields, 93.9\% within $\pm$1 on fidelity scores) are consistent with the DNA labeler results above.

\subsection{Research Questions for Selection Analysis}
\label{sec:selection-research-questions}

The enrichment data enables three lines of analysis. First, we compare the structural DNA of cited versus additional links to identify which content features predict inline citation versus supplementary attachment. Second, we track cross-run citation consistency: whether URLs demoted to ``additional'' in one run are promoted to ``cited'' in a different run of the same prompt, quantifying the stochasticity of the citation boundary (Section~\ref{sec:flip-flop}). Third, we compare the DNA of Bing Page~1 or Google Top 10 links that ChatGPT ignored against those it cited, testing whether ignored links exhibit lower structural complexity (Section~\ref{sec:cited-vs-serp}).

\section{Citation Mapping and Claim-Level Attribution}
\label{sec:citation-mapping}

This section describes how we map ChatGPT's written claims to their retrieved sources at claim-level resolution. The pipeline was applied specifically to listicle-cited claims, where the research question is sharpest: when a model cites a listicle containing 10+ products, does it actually extract information from that page, or does it hallucinate from parametric knowledge and merely attach the listicle as a plausible-looking source? We further restricted the analysis to \textbf{solo-cited claims}---claim blocks attributed to exactly one URL---because when multiple sources back a single claim, it becomes impossible to determine which source the model actually drew from. This filter was applied across both GPT and Gemini.

\subsection{The Claim-to-Link Forensic Pipeline}
\label{sec:claim-to-link}

Unlike Gemini, which natively provides segment-level attribution via \texttt{groundingSupports} (Section~\ref{sec:grounding-metadata}), ChatGPT exposes no such mapping. We therefore developed a forensic reconstruction pipeline that achieves comparable resolution through four steps. First, raw citation tokens (e.g., \texttt{citeturn0search9}) are extracted from the network event stream along with their character positions (\texttt{start\_idx}/\texttt{end\_idx}) in the streamed response. Second, the script identifies the full claim block---the descriptive text between consecutive citation tokens---capturing the complete product description attributed to that source. For example, a single claim block might read: \emph{``InqScribe---desktop transcription software with a perpetual license (no subscription; runs locally)''} mapped to \texttt{citeturn0search24} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{inqscribe.com}. Third, each claim block is joined to its retrieved ground truth (snippet, title, and URL from \texttt{search\_result\_groups}) by resolving \texttt{ref\_id} keys. Fourth, the pipeline produces a structured \texttt{mapping.json} per run enabling the same claim-level analyses that Gemini's API provides natively. This pipeline is what makes direct GPT-vs-Gemini comparison possible at the claim level.

\subsection{Multi-Chip Reconstruction}
\label{sec:multi-chip}

We observed cases where ChatGPT groups multiple sources under a single citation display (e.g., ``Maestra AI+1''). Our mapping revealed that these correspond to concatenated citation tokens (e.g., \texttt{turn0search8} + \texttt{search15}), meaning a single claim block is grounded by two or more distinct URLs simultaneously. We documented the prevalence of these multi-cited claims (Section~\ref{sec:solo-cited}) but excluded them from the downstream listicle fidelity analysis, which is restricted to solo-cited claims---those backed by exactly one URL---where the source of a given claim is unambiguous. How models synthesize information across multiple grounding sources remains an open question for future work.

\subsection{Definitions for Multi-Source Analysis}
\label{sec:multi-source-definitions}

A \textbf{multi-cited claim} is a claim or segment backed by 2+ distinct cited URLs. Among multi-cited claims, a \textbf{mixed listicle+product citation} is a case where the cited URL set contains at least one listicle and at least one product page. Each multi-cited claim is partitioned into exactly one bucket: mixed listicle+product, listicle-only, product-only, or other. Quantitative results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:solo-cited}.

\section{Localization and Retrieval Environment}
\label{sec:localization}

Geographical context affects retrieval in ways that are not always visible to the user. This section defines our localization taxonomy and documents the cross-language fan-out patterns we observed.

\subsection{Localization Definitions and Language Distribution}
\label{sec:localization-definitions}

Our dataset consists of 72 English prompts and 7 foreign-language prompts (1~French, 1~Chinese, 2~Turkish, 1~German, 1~Italian, 1~Spanish). All 7 foreign-language prompts originated from Ahrefs keyword clusters; the Profound-sourced prompts were exclusively English.

We distinguish two forms of localization. \textbf{Explicit localization} occurs when the query targets an inherently local category---even without a geographic qualifier, a query like ``best bakery'' triggers location-injected fan-out queries (e.g., ``best bakery near Munich Germany''). This primarily applies to local-commerce queries and is not a factor in our SaaS/software dataset. \textbf{Implicit localization} occurs when the search engine uses the user's IP, browser language, or search history to localize results for a general query.

In our fan-out data, implicit localization manifests as one of the two fan-out queries being rewritten into the prompt's local language. For example, when issuing an English prompt from Munich, Germany, we observed one query in English (\texttt{"free website or program to translate video and add subtitles"}) and a parallel query in German (\texttt{"kostenlos video \"ubersetzen und Untertitel automatisch hinzuf\"ugen\ldots"})---routing half the retrieval into the German-language index despite an English user prompt.

\subsection{Cross-Language Fan-Out Patterns}

We identified 62 GPT runs (13.1\% of 474) with non-English fan-out queries, distributed across three distinct patterns. These observations align with independent findings by \textcite{rudzki2026english}, who document at scale that ChatGPT searches in English even when the user prompt is in another language:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Case~1---Foreign-language prompts (30 runs):} When the user prompt is in a non-English language (e.g., P019~French, P038~Turkish, P056~Italian), GPT emits fan-out queries in both the prompt's language and English. Gemini exhibits the same pattern. This is expected behavior---the system mirrors the prompt language while anchoring to English for broader coverage.
    \item \textbf{Case~2---Context-triggered (9 runs):} English prompts that mention a specific language or locale trigger non-English fan-out queries. For example, ``best software for Parsian/Farsi audio-to-text transcription'' (P058) produces Farsi queries; ``Chrome extension for live Chinese to English translation'' (P047) produces Chinese queries. The non-English query is contextually motivated by the prompt's subject matter.
    \item \textbf{Case~3---Untriggered (23 runs):} Purely English prompts with no language or locale context produce non-English fan-out queries. For example, ``Is there a real time audio to text translation?'' (P007) generates Chinese and Japanese queries. These appear driven by IP/locale signals leaking into query generation.
