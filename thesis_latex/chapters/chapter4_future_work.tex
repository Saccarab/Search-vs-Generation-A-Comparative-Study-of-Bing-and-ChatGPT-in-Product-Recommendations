\chapter{Limitations, Research Gaps \& Future Work}
\label{ch:future-work}

This study traces the retrieval-to-citation pipeline for ChatGPT and Gemini on commercial product queries. The findings are robust within that scope, but several limitations constrain their interpretation, and several extensions would strengthen generalisability, deepen causal understanding, and address gaps we could not resolve within the current design.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Observational Design}
\label{sec:lim-observational}

This study is observational, not experimental. We measure correlations between structural features and citation selection, but we cannot establish causation. A page with tables may be cited more often because the model prefers tables, because table-containing pages tend to be higher quality, or because table-containing pages rank higher in the SERP. A true causal test would require creating matched page variants at controlled SERP positions, a design that is not feasible with production search engines. More broadly, we measure what the model cites but not how users respond to those citations; whether LLM-referred users convert at higher rates than traditional search traffic remains an open question.

\subsection{Dataset Scope}
\label{sec:lim-runs}
\label{sec:lim-domain}
\label{sec:lim-language}
\label{sec:lim-fidelity}

With 79 prompts and 3 runs per prompt per tier, the dataset is sufficient to identify broad patterns but limits per-prompt precision. All prompts target AI and SaaS products, a deliberate choice that enabled deeper qualitative analysis (Section~\ref{sec:query-set-selection}) but means the structural selection signals we observe may be partly driven by software-comparison content rather than universal LLM preferences. Only 7 prompts are non-English; Cases~2 and~3 (English prompts receiving foreign-language fan-outs, Section~\ref{sec:localization-bias}) are novel observations we have not encountered in prior work, making larger-scale corroboration particularly valuable. The semantic fidelity audit is also restricted to solo-cited claims; multi-cited claims (10.7\% of GPT, 34.5\% of Gemini; Section~\ref{sec:solo-cited}) were excluded and may exhibit different error patterns. More prompts, more runs per prompt, greater category and language diversity, and coverage of additional ChatGPT tiers and other LLM products would all strengthen the findings. Our study covers only ChatGPT Business and Personal; the free tier uses a structurally different search classifier with an inverted default (Section~\ref{sec:search-trigger}), and the Sonic Classifier metadata is no longer exposed in the network event stream as of late February~2026, meaning future replication will require alternative instrumentation.

\subsection{SERP Baselines and Instrumentation Asymmetry}
\label{sec:lim-serp}
\label{sec:lim-gemini}
\label{sec:lim-search-trigger}

We scraped Bing's consumer UI via a browser extension (subject to the Page~1 truncation artifact in Section~\ref{sec:bing-pagination-instability}) and collected roughly 3 pages of Google organic results via SerpApi. The LLMs receive results through backend APIs, not the consumer interfaces we scraped, so our overlap measurements compare citations against a \emph{proxy} for the retrieval surface; the reported invisible rate (16--22\%) is best interpreted as an upper bound. An additional asymmetry: ChatGPT was studied through the production consumer UI while Gemini was studied through the Vertex~AI API, which provides structured grounding metadata but does not carry IP or locale signals (Section~\ref{sec:gemini-instrumentation-differences}). Some cross-system differences may therefore reflect instrumentation rather than genuine model-level divergence.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}
\label{sec:fw-replication}
\label{sec:fw-methodology}
\label{sec:fw-search-trigger}
\label{sec:fw-user-study}

The limitations above point to several natural extensions. Deeper and repeated SERP crawling would tighten bounds on the invisible citation rate, more runs per prompt would distinguish stable brands from marginal ones, and replication across product categories and languages would test whether our structural signals generalise beyond AI and SaaS. Re-running the same queries at fixed intervals would reveal whether citation patterns drift as models, indices, and web content evolve. Extending to other systems, particularly Perplexity \parencite{ahrefs2026chatgptvsgoogle} and Google AI Overviews, would test whether our findings are universal properties of RAG-based search.

On the methodological side, testing rank and structural features together in a single model rather than independently would help separate which signals actually drive citation from which ones just correlate with position. Publication date, available in ChatGPT's retrieval metadata for about half of URLs, could be tested as an additional signal.

Reasoning-mode variants (ChatGPT's ``thinking'' mode, Gemini's extended thinking) may use different retrieval pipelines and could produce different citation behaviour than the standard generation mode we studied. Why the model sometimes issues additional search turns (Section~\ref{sec:three-layer}) also remains unexplained.
