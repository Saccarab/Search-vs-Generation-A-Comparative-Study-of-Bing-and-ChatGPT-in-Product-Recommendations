  LLMs are rapidly becoming the interface through which consumers discover products. By mid-2025, ChatGPT alone had reached approximately 10\% of the world's adult population, with information-seeking and practical guidance accounting for nearly 80\% of all conversations \parencite{chatterji2025chatgpt}. Yet especially for commercial intent prompts these models do not generate recommendations from memory alone anymore. When a user asks for a product recommendation, the system can search the web in real time, retrieve relevant sources, and ground its response in what
  it finds. This retrieval-and-grounding workflow is commonly referred to as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and it is
  increasingly standard in LLM conversations when up-to-date information is required. The model supplies the reasoning; the web supplies the knowledge.

The numbers are stark: Google alone commands over 100$\times$ the referral traffic of all AI assistants combined. However, the growth trajectory is noteworthy. \section{The Commercial Catalyst for RAG}
\label{sec:commercial-catalyst}

Not all queries are equally affected by the shift to RAG from basic inference.  Grounding is more likely to happen when the user's intent demands real-time, verifiable information (prices, availability, feature comparisons, reviews, local information) that parametric knowledge cannot reliably provide. Product recommendations sit squarely in this category: they require freshness, competitive breadth, and factual precision, making them the segment where the retrieval pipeline is most active and where the mechanics of source selection are most commercially consequential. \subsection{The Emerging GEO Industry}
\label{sec:geo-landscape}

The commercial significance of LLM citation behavior has already attracted substantial venture capital. In August 2025, Profound raised \$35~million in a Series~B round led by Sequoia Capital, bringing its total funding to \$58.5~million \parencite{fortune2025profound}. Three months later, Berlin-based Peec~AI raised \$21~million in a Series~A led by Singular \parencite{techcrunch2025peec}, growing to \$4M~ARR in 10~months with 1,300 companies on its platform. Both companies are building brand-visibility dashboards that track how often a brand appears in LLM-generated responses and track the sentiment AI agents use when mentioning brands.

Ahrefs, one of the largest SEO analytics platforms, has also added AI search monitoring features that track brand visibility across LLM-generated responses alongside its traditional SERP tracking. Adobe announced the acquisition of SEMrush in November 2025 \parencite{adobe2025semrush}, a deal that signals how seriously the marketing technology industry takes the convergence of traditional keyword intelligence and AI search analytics. At their core, these platforms are trying to answer a simple question: \emph{is the AI recommending your brand, and if not, why?} Our findings speak directly to what drives these outcomes. 


The dominant industry narrative frames GEO as a replacement for SEO, a new discipline for a new era. However, the empirical question this thesis investigates is whether GEO is genuinely distinct from SEO or whether it is, in practice, a thin optimization layer on top of the same ranking foundation. The answer, as the following chapters demonstrate, is substantially the latter, but with empirically real exceptions that the concluding chapter delineates (Section~\ref{sec:geo-not-seo}).

\section{Background}
\label{sec:theoretical-framework}

\subsection{From SEO to GEO}
\label{sec:evolution-search}
Search Engine Optimization (SEO) is the established practice of optimizing content for visibility in organic search results through content, external website link-based authority signals, and technical performance. As LLMs increasingly mediate search interactions, a similar optimization practice is emerging. The industry has not converged on a single term for this; common labels include GEO (Generative Engine Optimization), AEO (Answer Engine Optimization), AI~SEO and AIO (AI Optimization). Throughout this thesis we adopt \textbf{GEO} following \textcite{aggarwal2024geo}, as it is the most established term in academic literature, but the underlying concept is the same across labels: optimizing content so that it survives the retrieval-to-citation pipeline. The following subsections trace how retrieval became central to LLM answer generation, and what that means for optimization.

\subsection{The Rise of RAG}
\label{sec:rag-evolution}

Early LLMs relied purely on parametric knowledge: static information frozen at the time of training. This produced two common failure modes: hallucination (confident generation of false claims) and data staleness (inability to reflect prices, reviews, or product launches that post-date the training cutoff). The fundamental insight that led to RAG was that LLMs are better at \emph{reasoning} (logic, synthesis, formatting) than at being a database \parencite{lewis2020rag}. Rather than encoding all world knowledge in model weights, RAG decouples reasoning from knowledge by introducing a three-stage pipeline: the model first \emph{retrieves} relevant documents from an external source (typically a search engine), the retrieved text is \emph{augmented} into the model's context window as grounding chunks, and the model then \emph{generates} a response by reasoning over the provided evidence.

RAG became the industry standard for three reinforcing reasons. First, it provides a citation trail: every claim can be traced to a retrievable source, enabling at least partial verification. Second, it is efficient: smaller models augmented with retrieval routinely outperform larger models operating from parametric knowledge alone, because the retrieval step offloads the burden of memorization. Third, it allows freshness: no model can be retrained hourly to keep pace with the web, but a search index can be queried in real time.

\subsection{The ``Tiny Model'' Vision}
\label{sec:rag-economics}
\label{sec:reasoning-models}

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman described this vision at Snowflake Summit 2025: \textit{``a very tiny model with superhuman reasoning, 1 trillion tokens of context, and access to every tool you can imagine''}\footnote{Source: ``Sam Altman Talks AGI Timeline \& Next-Gen AI Capabilities,'' Snowflake Summit 2025. Available at \url{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qhnJDDX2hhU}.}. In this vision, the model does not need to contain knowledge, only the ability to reason over externally provided information. The web becomes a distributed memory layer for AI orchestrators, queried on demand rather than compressed into weights.

This approach is also economically inevitable. Inference (the computational cost of LLM ``thinking'') is orders of magnitude more expensive than retrieval via traditional search indexing. It cannot produce as fresh results as the web that changes by the minute. Retrieval, by contrast, is cheap, real-time. The economic logic is clear: let the model reason, and let the search index remember. The practical consequence for this thesis is equally clear: if retrieval is the foundation of AI-generated answers, then what gets retrieved creates the baseline for what gets recommended.

\subsection{The Emerging ``Agent Web''}
\label{sec:agent-web}

As retrieval becomes central to AI-generated answers, some infrastructure providers are beginning to optimize content delivery for agents themselves. Cloudflare, one of the largest CDN and web infrastructure providers, introduced a ``Markdown for Agents'' feature \parencite{martinho2026markdown} that uses HTTP content negotiation to serve markdown instead of HTML when AI crawlers request a page. \textcite{punturo2026markdown} tested markdown serving in a controlled A/B experiment across 381~pages and found no statistically significant advantage over standard HTML in AI bot traffic. 
Other proposals like \texttt{llms.txt} \parencite{howard2024llmstxt}, a markdown site map for LLMs inspired by the traditional \texttt{robots.txt} standard (which tells search engine crawlers which pages they should index on a website), have seen growing adoption but similarly lack empirical evidence of improving AI citation rates \parencite{seranking2025llmstxt}.
There are many examples around the web of content creation such as Sana Labs' LMS comparison\footnote{\url{https://sanalabs.com/learn-blog/best-lms-software-on-the-market-2025-top-platforms-reviewed}} which are structured almost entirely around comparison tables, standardized per-product templates with bullet-pointed strengths and limitations, and FAQ sections. This kind of content, by the page's own description, is optimized for AI assistants and LLM search. Whether this kind of structural richness affects LLM citation likelihood is one of the questions we investigate in Section~\ref{sec:selection-drift}.

Regardless of their effectiveness, the fact that these efforts exist tells us something about the trajectory the web is on: one layer optimized for human readers, another increasingly shaped by the needs of AI agents that crawl, retrieve, and cite it.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Citation Fidelity in RAG Systems}
\label{sec:rw-fidelity}

\textcite{wu2025sourcecheckup} developed SourceCheckup, an automated verification framework that evaluated 7~LLMs across 800~medical questions, generating 58,000 statement--source pairs validated by physicians. Their central finding is that retrieval substantially reduces citation errors: GPT-4o with web search achieved $\sim$70\% statement-level support versus substantially worse rates for parametric-only models. Our study extends this into a different domain (commercial product recommendations) on newer model generations (GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Flash), with a narrower but deeper focus: rather than scoring all statements in a response, we audit individual product claims against their solo-cited listicle sources and manually verify every flagged case (Section~\ref{sec:listicle-bias}).

\subsection{Large-Scale Observational Studies}
\label{sec:rw-observational}

\textcite{punturo2026citationsources} analyzed 730,000 U.S.\ English-language ChatGPT conversations (October--December 2025), reporting an overall 18\% web search trigger rate across all intents, rising to 53.5\% for commercial queries (Figure~\ref{fig:trigger-rate}), with approximately 4 unique citations per search-triggered turn. Our study, by deliberately targeting high commercial intent prompts, observes a substantially higher trigger rate of 89\% and citation density of 9.94 per turn, consistent with Profound's own finding that commercial queries activate the retrieval pipeline far more aggressively.

\textcite{rudzki2026english} analyzed 10M+ ChatGPT prompts and 20M+ fan-out queries (the multiple search queries an LLM issues behind the scenes for a single user prompt) via Peec~AI's production monitoring, finding that 78\% of non-English sessions include at least one English sub-search and 43\% of all fan-out queries are issued in English regardless of the prompt's language. The cross-language fan-out patterns they document (Case~1: foreign prompt $\rightarrow$ English retrieval) are directly corroborated in our data; we additionally identify the reverse direction (Cases~2--3: English prompt $\rightarrow$ foreign-language fan out queries), which has not been previously documented (Section~\ref{sec:localization-bias}).

\subsection{Grounding Mechanics and the Content Budget}
\label{sec:rw-grounding-mechanics}

Another important question \textcite{petrovic2025grounding} tries to answer is how much of a retrieved page actually makes it into the model's context. The study measured Gemini's grounding chunks and found that the system operates with a fixed content budget of approximately 2,000 words per query, with the top-ranked source receiving 28\% of the budget and pages over 3,000 words achieving only 13\% coverage. \textcite{petrovic2025filter} further quantified the compression ratio between retrieved content and generated output: only 15--25\% of retrieved text survives into the final response. \textcite{indig2026attention} analyzed 1.2~million ChatGPT responses and found a ``ski ramp'' distribution where 44.2\% of citations originate from the first 30\% of a source page. 

We conduct a similar analysis within our listicle position bias results (Section~\ref{sec:reranking}): if the model's grounding window is limited, items listed later in a long listicle may never enter the context. While we do not measure grounding budgets directly, our study provides complementary evidence from the selection side: we observe the downstream effect (position-dependent uptake rates) that these budget constraints appear to produce.
Prior work has audited citation fidelity (but not selection mechanics), measured citation patterns at scale (but without access to retrieval pipelines), and quantified grounding budgets (but not the downstream selection effects). This thesis attempts to connect these threads by tracing the retrieval-to-citation pipeline across multiple LLMs and tiers (ChatGPT Business vs.\ Personal, and Gemini), with particular attention to within-listicle position bias, structural content features that predict citation, citations that fall outside search indices entirely, and claim-level attribution.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

\textit{RQ1: How does grounding behavior manifest in LLM-generated product recommendations, and how does it vary across deployment tiers and providers?}

This core question decomposes into four sub-questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item RQ1a (Selection and visibility): How do cited, additional, rejected, and invisible sources differ in domain and type, and how does this differ between Business and Personal tiers?
    \item RQ1b (External support): How often do LLM citations appear in conventional search engine results (Bing and Google)?
    \item RQ1c (Selection bias): Do models exhibit preferences for specific structural content features (e.g., tables, numbered lists, freshness cues) when selecting from the retrieved pool, and how do these preferences vary across systems?
    \item RQ1d (Listicle fidelity): When listicles (list-format articles or blog posts such as "Top 10 Best...") are cited, which products within them are selected or ignored, and how faithfully does the model reproduce their details?
\end{enumerate}

To answer these questions, we compare LLM citations against two external search indices. Bing serves as the primary overlap baseline for ChatGPT, while Google provides a second overlap index and the retrieval baseline for Gemini. Then we analyze the characteristics of the pages that made it to the citations sections of these LLM responses and compare them against the ones that did not.
