Large language models are rapidly becoming the interface through which consumers discover products. By mid-2025, ChatGPT alone had reached approximately 10\% of the world's adult population, with information-seeking and practical guidance accounting for nearly 80\% of all conversations \parencite{chatterji2025chatgpt}. \subsubsection{Operational Definition (What ``Grounding Behavior'' Means in This Thesis)}
\label{sec:grounding-definition}

Grounding behavior is the measurable pipeline from \textbf{retrieval $\rightarrow$ selection $\rightarrow$ citation $\rightarrow$ final text}, using observable artifacts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Retrieved candidate set} (where available): sources the system pulled/considered
    \item \textbf{Selected set}: sources that ``survive'' selection (cited/attached)
    \item \textbf{Claim linkage}: which textual claims map to which sources (claim-to-link mapping)
    \item \textbf{SERP support}: whether selected sources are actually present in external SERPs (Bing/Google)
    \item \textbf{Source-to-output fidelity}: whether products mentioned in retrieved listicles are carried into the final recommendations
\end{itemize}

\section{Theoretical Framework: From SEO to GEO}
\label{sec:theoretical-framework}

\subsection{Search Optimization in the Age of Generative AI}
\label{sec:evolution-search}

Search Engine Optimization (SEO) is the established practice of optimizing content for visibility in organic search results through relevance, authority signals, and technical performance. As LLMs increasingly mediate search interactions, a parallel optimization practice is emerging: tailoring content to be retrieved, selected, and cited within AI-generated answers. The industry has not converged on a single term for this---common labels include GEO (Generative Engine Optimization), AEO (Answer Engine Optimization), AI~SEO, AIO (AI Optimization), and LLMO. Throughout this thesis we adopt \textbf{GEO} following \textcite{aggarwal2024geo}, as it is the most established term in academic literature, but the underlying concept is the same across labels: optimizing content so that it survives the retrieval-to-citation pipeline of RAG-based search systems.

Understanding why GEO exists---and why it does not replace SEO---requires tracing the architectural evolution that made retrieval central to how LLMs generate answers.

\subsection{The Rise of Retrieval-Augmented Generation}
\label{sec:rag-evolution}

Early LLMs relied purely on parametric knowledge: static information frozen at the time of training. This produced two well-documented failure modes---hallucination (confident generation of false claims) and data staleness (inability to reflect prices, reviews, or product launches that post-date the training cutoff). The fundamental insight that led to Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) was that LLMs are better at \emph{reasoning}---logic, synthesis, formatting---than at being a database \parencite{lewis2020rag}. [idk about this bit review maybe] Rather than encoding all world knowledge in model weights, RAG decouples reasoning from knowledge by introducing a three-stage pipeline: the model first \emph{retrieves} relevant documents from an external source (typically a search engine), the retrieved text is \emph{augmented} into the model's context window as grounding chunks, and the model then \emph{generates} a response by reasoning over the provided evidence.

RAG became the industry standard for three reinforcing reasons. First, it provides a citation trail: every claim can be traced to a retrievable source, enabling at least partial verification. Second, it is efficient: smaller models augmented with retrieval routinely outperform larger models operating from parametric knowledge alone, because the retrieval step offloads the burden of memorization. Third, it is the only scalable mechanism for freshness---no model can be retrained hourly to keep pace with the web, but a search index can be queried in real time.

\subsection{The ``Tiny Model'' Vision and the Economics of Retrieval}
\label{sec:rag-economics}
\label{sec:reasoning-models}

The logical endpoint of this architecture was articulated by Sam Altman at Snowflake Summit 2025: \textit{``a very tiny model with superhuman reasoning, 1 trillion tokens of context, and access to every tool you can imagine''}\footnote{Source: ``Sam Altman Talks AGI Timeline \& Next-Gen AI Capabilities,'' Snowflake Summit 2025. Available at \url{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qhnJDDX2hhU}.}. In this vision, the model does not need to contain knowledge---only the ability to reason over externally provided information. The web becomes a distributed memory layer for AI orchestrators, queried on demand rather than compressed into weights.

This vision is not aspirational; it is economically inevitable. Inference---the computational cost of LLM ``thinking''---is orders of magnitude more expensive than retrieval via traditional search indexing. Training encodes knowledge at enormous one-time cost and cannot keep pace with a web that changes by the minute. Retrieval, by contrast, is cheap, real-time, and already built at planetary scale by search engines. The economic logic is clear: let the model reason, and let the search index remember. The practical consequence for this thesis is equally clear: if retrieval is the foundation of AI-generated answers, then what gets retrieved determines what gets recommended---and what gets retrieved is governed by search engine ranking.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

Research on how LLMs select and cite web sources is young---most production RAG systems with grounding launched in 2024--2025---and the empirical literature remains thin. The work closest to ours falls into three categories: citation fidelity audits, large-scale observational studies of citation patterns, and investigations of the grounding mechanics that sit between retrieval and generation. \subsection{Citation Fidelity in RAG Systems}
\label{sec:rw-fidelity}

\subsection{Grounding Mechanics and the Content Budget}
\label{sec:rw-grounding-mechanics}

A growing body of practitioner research examines \emph{how much} of a retrieved page actually enters the model's context. \textcite{petrovic2025grounding} measured Google's grounding chunks and found that the system operates with a fixed content budget of approximately 2,000 words per query, with the top-ranked source receiving 28\% of the budget and pages over 3,000 words achieving only 13\% coverage. \textcite{petrovic2025filter} further quantified the compression ratio between retrieved content and generated output: only 15--25\% of retrieved text survives into the final response. \textcite{indig2026attention} analyzed 1.2~million ChatGPT responses and found a ``ski ramp'' distribution where 44.2\% of citations originate from the first 30\% of a source page ($p < 0.0001$ over 18,012 verified citations), with citation likelihood correlating with definitive language and entity density.

\subsection{The ``Agent Web'' and Format Optimization}
\label{sec:rw-agent-web}

A parallel line of work asks whether serving content in machine-optimized formats improves AI visibility. \textcite{martinho2026markdown} describe Cloudflare's ``Markdown for Agents'' feature, which uses HTTP content negotiation to serve markdown instead of HTML when AI crawlers request a page, reducing token consumption by up to 80\%.
